\section{Controllers}
\label{sec:controllers}

\subsection{Flight: the ideal boid is not the robot}
\label{sec:flight}

The first version turned Reynolds' accelerations straight into a tilt.
Twenty drones hovered, then neighbouring pairs swung in and out, wider each
time, until two touched: a stiff separation rule behind a slow, underdamped
attitude controller (a tilt step overshot by 45\%) oscillates, as Virágh et
al.\ predict. The robot was fixed before the rules, with the flight layer of
\cref{fig:flight}, whose attitude loops follow the Crazyflie firmware
\citep{bitcraze2024}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the \emph{velocity layer} turns every model's output, a wanted
        velocity $\mathbf v_d$, into an acceleration that closes the gap
        with time constant $\tau = 0.3$\,s, capped at 2.5\,m/s$^2$; 0.3\,s is
        the fastest response measured without overshoot, below it the
        attitude loops can't keep up;
  \item the acceleration becomes a tilt, held by two nested loops: the
        \emph{angle loop} asks for a rotation rate, capped so that hard
        reversals don't flip the drone, and the \emph{rate loop} sets the
        motor differential from the gyro. A tilt step now settles in 0.26\,s
        with 4\% overshoot, and Reynolds' rules, unchanged, settled into a
        calm lattice.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Three flocking models}
\label{sec:models}

Each model answers the same question: given what a drone hears of its
neighbours, which way and how fast should it fly? Notation: $\mathbf d_{ij}$
is the offset from drone $i$ to neighbour $j$ and $d_{ij}$ its length,
$\hat{\mathbf x}$ is the unit vector along $\mathbf x$, and a bar is the mean
over the neighbours within 2.5\,m. For a leader, $\hat{\mathbf g}_i$ is the
direction to go. $v_0 = 1$\,m/s is the cruise speed; leaders slow down over
the last 3\,m to C. The models share everything else.

\paragraph{Reynolds.} Four accelerations, added up: away from neighbours that
are too close, toward the neighbours' mean velocity, toward their centre,
and, for leaders only, toward the goal at cruise speed:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf a_i =
  \underbrace{k_s \!\!\sum_{d_{ij} < r_s}\!\! \Bigl(\frac{r_s}{d_{ij}} - 1\Bigr)(-\hat{\mathbf d}_{ij})}_{\text{separation}}
  + \underbrace{k_a \bigl(\overline{\mathbf v}_j - \mathbf v_i\bigr)}_{\text{alignment}}
  + \underbrace{k_c\, \overline{\mathbf d}_{ij}}_{\text{cohesion}}
  + \underbrace{k_g\,(v_0 \hat{\mathbf g}_i - \mathbf v_i)}_{\text{seek (leaders)}}.
\end{equation}
Separation grows sharply as a neighbour comes within $r_s = 1$\,m. Note what
is missing: nothing pushes a follower forward, it moves only as its
neighbours pull it. Weights: $k_s = 4$, $k_a = 4$, $k_c = 1.2$, $k_g = 4$.

\paragraph{Couzin.} Each drone keeps a direction of its own,
$\hat{\mathbf u}_i$, and always flies it at $v_0$. Every step it updates the
direction with a strict priority: if any neighbour is closer than
$\alpha = 0.4$\,m, it only turns away; otherwise every neighbour counts the
same, pulling it toward their positions and their directions:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf u_i \leftarrow
  \begin{cases}
    \underbrace{-\textstyle\sum_{d_{ij} < \alpha} \hat{\mathbf d}_{ij}}_{\text{turn away}}
      & \text{if any } d_{ij} < \alpha,\\[12pt]
    \underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{j} \hat{\mathbf d}_{ij}}_{\text{toward them}}
      + \underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{j} \hat{\mathbf v}_j}_{\text{their directions}}
      + \underbrace{\hat{\mathbf u}_i}_{\text{its own}}
      & \text{otherwise.}
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
A leader then turns toward the goal, $\hat{\mathbf u}_i \leftarrow
\mathrm{unit}(\hat{\mathbf u}_i + \omega \hat{\mathbf g}_i)$ with
$\omega = 1$, so it is as much drawn by the goal as by its own heading.

\paragraph{Hybrid.} Built on Vásárhelyi et al.'s model for real drones, with
Reynolds' cohesion and Couzin's leaders. Its four terms are velocities:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf v_d =
  \underbrace{v_0 \hat{\mathbf h}_i}_{\text{cruise}}
  + \underbrace{p_r \!\!\sum_{d_{ij} < r_0}\!\! (r_0 - d_{ij})(-\hat{\mathbf d}_{ij})}_{\text{repulsion}}
  + \underbrace{C_f \sum_j \bigl(|\mathbf v_{ij}| - D_{ij}\bigr)^{+}\, \hat{\mathbf v}_{ij}}_{\text{friction}}
  + \underbrace{p_c\, \overline{\mathbf d}_{ij}}_{\text{cohesion}}.
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Cruise}: every drone flies at full speed along
        $\hat{\mathbf h}_i$, the mean direction of the neighbours it sees
        moving. A leader mixes that direction with the goal,
        $\mathrm{unit}(\hat{\mathbf h}_i + \hat{\mathbf g}_i)$.
  \item \emph{Repulsion} grows linearly as a neighbour comes within
        $r_0 = 1$\,m: softer than Reynolds', which is what a lagging robot
        needs.
  \item \emph{Friction} is alignment with a tolerance: a neighbour's
        velocity difference $\mathbf v_{ij}$ is corrected only by the part
        above an allowed $D_{ij}$, small up close and large far away. So
        close neighbours match velocities, distant ones may differ.
\end{itemize}
Three departures from Vásárhelyi's model each answer a failure on
\texttt{l\_hall}: friction weighted 0.2 instead of 1, since summed over ten
neighbours it asked more than the drone could fly and the flock jiggled; the
stopping limit below instead of walls that push, which bounced the flock off
the pillar forest; and a heading taken from the neighbours instead of each
drone's own, since eighteen private headings outvoted two leaders.

\paragraph{Stopping limit.} Shared by all models, it removes speed and
never adds any. Toward a wall, a drone may fly only as fast as it could
still stop 0.3\,m short; toward a neighbour, the same, on top of how fast
that neighbour is moving away. Nothing is pushed, so nothing bounces.

\paragraph{Tuning.} The hybrid was tuned throughout its development on
\texttt{l\_hall}. For fairness, Couzin and Reynolds were then tuned on the
same map under one protocol fixed in advance: two parameters each, 15 runs,
on seeds the experiments never use.

\subsection{The leaders' planning layer}
\label{sec:planner}

Planning was not part of the original design: leaders were to steer
straight at C, and the flock was to do the rest. The first flights of
Reynolds' flock on \texttt{l\_hall} showed why that fails. The straight
line to C runs through the wall at the L's inner corner, so a leader steering
along it is pressed into that wall and stops there, a local minimum like a
potential field's, and the flock stays with it; tuning later confirmed that no weights get Reynolds' leaders round. A
comparison of models would then only measure which one happens to escape a
local minimum. So the leaders were given a deliberative layer on top of the
reactive model, the hybrid architecture of the course: a leader plans a path
on the known map and follows it, while the model stays as it is and the
followers are untouched. The \texttt{dead\_end} map was built with it, to
make the trap explicit: the straight line from A to C ends in the bottom of
a cup.

\paragraph{Path finding.} A* on a 0.25\,m grid around every obstacle at
least 1\,m across (smaller ones, like the pillars, are left to the stopping
limit). No single clearance works: a large one erases the passages, a small
one drags the flock along every corner. So clearance is a cost: cells within
0.3\,m of an obstacle are forbidden, and steps get more expensive the closer
they come to one, up to 2\,m away. The path keeps to the middle of halls and
gaps (\cref{fig:maps}) and is pruned to its corners.

\paragraph{Path following.} The leader aims at the point 2\,m ahead along its
path (pure pursuit) and hands the model that direction, so no model had to
change. If the flock pushes a leader behind a wall, so that the point ahead
is out of sight, the leader plans again from where it is.

\paragraph{Each leader on its own.} Every leader plans for itself, from where
it stands, and this is deliberate. Leaders cannot recognise each other any
more than the followers can recognise them, so they cannot exchange plans,
as in a real swarm of identical drones with no leader flag. Where an
obstacle can be passed on either side, two leaders may therefore choose
different sides.
