\Cref{tab:verdicts} sums up the hypotheses. The research question, H4, holds
for one model out of three. One property explains most of the results: what
makes a follower move.
\begin{description}
  \item[Hybrid: its own cruise.] Followers cruise at full speed along the
    neighbours' mean heading, so the flock has momentum. On \texttt{l\_turn}
    it carries the leaders round the corner without a plan; on
    \texttt{dead\_end} it carries the flock between two disagreeing leaders
    into the cup. This is the averaging Couzin et al.\ found when two
    informed subgroups prefer directions that differ little: two paths that
    part slowly start out almost parallel.
  \item[Reynolds: nothing but its neighbours.] Followers move only when
    pulled. With leaders stuck at a wall the flock stops with them; with
    leaders that plan, it follows faithfully, commits late, and goes with one
    leader when two disagree. One leader pulling is enough.
  \item[Couzin: a direction to be outvoted.] Followers keep a direction of
    their own, so leaders must outvote them: more leaders work better, as
    Couzin predicted, and one leader among 30 is lost.
\end{description}
No model is simply best: each one's strength and weakness come from the same
property, and the right choice depends on what the leaders can be trusted to
do.

\paragraph{A plan helps only if the flock follows.} The planning layer works
for the leaders: in the replays, no leader stayed stuck once it could replan.
Leaders plan independently by design, since they can't recognise each other
to share a plan, and two of them can choose different ways round an
obstacle. That was expected to split the flock at worst, each part with a
leader, and in Couzin's and Reynolds' models it does no more than that. In
a model that averages its leaders the same disagreement is worse than a
split: the hybrid succeeds in 7 of 10 runs whose leaders happen to choose the
same side, and in 2 of 20 whose leaders don't. So the cost of leaders that
can't communicate depends on the model of the followers.

\paragraph{Reliability against safety.} The most reliable setting,
Reynolds with the plan, is also the safest, about 0.5\,m between drones. The
hybrid and Couzin reach touching distance: the stopping limit keeps every
drone off the walls, but between drones it is one-sided and applied a time
constant late. On real drones, with downwash and noisier sensing, spacing
would weigh as much as success, as \citet{soria2021} argue for reactive swarms
in clutter.

\paragraph{Limitations.} Ten seeds resolve only large effects, and the $p$
values are uncorrected. The second experiment used one trap map, and the
split depends on an obstacle with two symmetric ways round. The hybrid had
more tuning than the others. Flight is flat, sensing idealised, downwash not
simulated. Why a 2--2 split does not break the hybrid is not explained.
