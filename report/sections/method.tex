\section{Experimental method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Hypotheses}
\label{sec:hypotheses}

\begin{description}
  \item[H1, planning.] Where the straight line from A to C crosses a wall,
    leaders that plan bring their flock to C more often than leaders that
    steer straight at C.
  \item[H2, the corner trap.] Reynolds' flock never gets round the L's inner
    corner with the plan off, and does with it.
  \item[H3, tuned Couzin.] Tuned like the hybrid, Couzin's model reaches C
    about as often, with wider spacing.
  \item[H4, leaders.] As Couzin et al.\ found in animal groups, success grows
    with the number of leaders $j$, for every model and flock size.
\end{description}

\subsection{Setting and measure}
\label{sec:setting}

\paragraph{Runs.} Two experiments, both on the held-out maps:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Evaluation} (H1--H3): 20 drones, 2 leaders, three models, both
        maps, plan off and on, 10 seeds: 120 runs.
  \item \emph{Drones and leaders} (H4): \texttt{dead\_end}, plan on,
        $n \in \{10, 20, 30\}$, $j \in \{0, 1, 2, 4\}$, three models, 10 seeds:
        360 runs, 30 shared with the evaluation, so 450 runs in all.
        $j = 0$ is the control: how often a flock reaches C by wandering.
\end{enumerate}
A seed fixes where the drones start and which ones lead, and every setting
runs on seeds 0--9, so runs pair by seed. Tuning used other seeds. Webots is
deterministic, so any run can be replayed and watched.

\paragraph{Success:} 90\% of the drones in C at once (not all: one drone cut
off at a corner should not fail a flock that arrived), within
$T = t_{\mathrm{go}} + 2L/v_0$, the go time plus twice what the leaders alone
need along their path: 83\,s on \texttt{dead\_end}, 112\,s on
\texttt{l\_turn}. A first evaluation with 200\,s let flocks that had lost
their leaders wander into C and count as successes of leadership; with $T$
the guided arrivals (47--63\,s) and the wanderers (108--148\,s) fall on
either side. \emph{Secondary:} time to 90\% in C, and the closest two
drones came during the run (centre to centre; 0.10\,m is touching).

\paragraph{Statistics.} Success is binary and paired by seed, so two settings
are compared with the exact McNemar test, which counts only the seeds where
one succeeds and the other fails; with 10 seeds the smallest possible $p$ is
0.002. Distances are compared with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, which
assumes no distribution, and unpaired groups with Fisher's exact test. The
$p$ values are uncorrected for the many comparisons, so they are read as
strength of evidence next to the counts, not as ``significant''.
