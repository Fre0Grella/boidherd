\section{Experimental method}
\label{sec:method}

The experiments follow the steps of \citet{nehmzow2009} and the course's
guidelines: state the hypotheses, fix the setting and the measure, then
analyse with statistics and plots. Each hypothesis below was written in the
design log before the runs that test it, with the date.

\subsection{Hypotheses}
\label{sec:hypotheses}

\begin{description}
  \item[H1, planning (15 Oct).] Where the straight line from A to C crosses a
    wall, leaders that plan bring their flock to C more often than leaders
    that steer straight at C; where it doesn't, the plan makes no difference.
  \item[H2, the corner trap (27 Oct).] No weights bring Reynolds' flock round
    the L's inner corner with the plan off, since the corner is a local
    minimum of steering straight at the goal; with the plan, it gets round.
  \item[H3, tuned Couzin (27 Oct).] Tuned on the same terms as the hybrid,
    Couzin's model reaches C about as often, and keeps wider spacing.
  \item[H4, leaders (14 Oct; for the sweep, 28 Oct).] The research question,
    from \citet{couzin2005}: more leaders bring the flock more reliably, and a
    larger flock needs a smaller fraction of leaders. Before the sweep: does
    each model's success grow with $j$ at every $n$?
\end{description}
A control comes with H4: with no leaders ($j = 0$), a flock can only reach
C by wandering, and how often it does within the time limit is the chance
level against which every success is read.

\subsection{Setting}
\label{sec:setting}

\paragraph{Maps.} All three models were tuned on \texttt{l\_hall}, so results
there would say how well each model fits the map it was tuned on. The
experiments run on the two maps no model was tuned on (\cref{fig:maps}):
\texttt{l\_turn}, where the straight line to C runs into the hall's wall at
the corner, and \texttt{dead\_end}, where it runs into the bottom of the cup.

\paragraph{Seeds.} A seed fixes where the drones start and which of them lead.
Every model and setting runs on the same seeds, 0 to 9, so runs pair by seed:
two models compared on seed 3 start from the same spawn, with the same
leaders. Tuning used seeds 100 and 101, so tuning and evaluation never share
a spawn. Webots runs are deterministic: a run replayed gives the same result
to the millisecond, so any failure can be replayed and watched.

\paragraph{Two experiments.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Evaluation} (H1--H3): 20 drones, 2 leaders, the three models
        on both maps with the plan off and on, 10 seeds: 120 runs.
  \item \emph{Drones and leaders} (H4): \texttt{dead\_end} with the plan on,
        $n \in \{10, 20, 30\}$, $j \in \{0, 1, 2, 4\}$, the three models, 10
        seeds: 360 runs, 30 of them the evaluation's own (20 drones, 2
        leaders, plan on). \texttt{dead\_end} was chosen because the
        evaluation showed it separates the models and \texttt{l\_turn} does
        not (\cref{sec:results}).
\end{enumerate}
All runs fly at 1\,m/s and one altitude; the leaders set off at 16\,s.

\subsection{Measure}
\label{sec:measure}

\paragraph{Success.} A run succeeds when 90\% of its drones are in C at once,
within the map's time limit. 90\% and not all: a single drone cut off at a
corner would otherwise fail a run in which the leaders brought the flock.

\paragraph{The time limit.} The first evaluation ran for 200\,s, and replays
showed that this let a flock that had lost its leaders wander into C by
chance: \texttt{dead\_end}'s room is closed, and C fills its far end. Those
runs counted as successes of leadership they weren't. Each run now ends at
the time a guided flock needs: the go time plus twice the time the leaders
alone would take along their planned path at the cruise speed,
\begin{equation}
  T = t_{\mathrm{go}} + 2\,L/v_0,
\end{equation}
which gives 83\,s on \texttt{dead\_end} ($L = 33.4$\,m) and 112\,s on
\texttt{l\_turn} ($L = 47.9$\,m). Twice and not a fixed margin, because
larger flocks take longer through a narrow passage, and a tight limit would
bias the results against large $n$. On the first evaluation the limit fell
between the two groups: guided arrivals on \texttt{dead\_end} took 47--63\,s,
the wanderers 108--148\,s. A tighter limit can still be applied afterwards,
from each run's time to 90\%.

\paragraph{Secondary measures.} The time to 90\% in C, for the runs that
succeed; the closest two drones came during the run, centre to centre
(a Crazyflie is 10\,cm across, so 0.10\,m is touching); crashes, a drone
that falls to the floor; and the drones in C at the limit, which tells a
near miss from a flock that never followed.

\subsection{Statistics}
\label{sec:statistics}

Success is binary and runs are paired by seed, so two settings are compared
with the exact McNemar test \citep{mcnemar1947}: only the seeds where one
setting succeeds and the other fails count, and under the null hypothesis
each is equally likely to go either way (a two-sided binomial test on those
seeds). With 10 seeds the smallest possible $p$ is 0.002, when all 10 differ
the same way. In the second experiment comparisons are also pooled over the
three flock sizes, 30 pairs. Continuous measures, such as the closest pair,
are shown as boxplots with every run drawn, and paired with the Wilcoxon
signed-rank test \citep{wilcoxon1945}, which assumes no distribution: results
of robot runs are rarely normal, and ten are too few to tell. Groups that are
not paired by seed, such as runs whose leaders took the same or different
ways, are compared with Fisher's exact test.

The $p$ values are not corrected for the number of comparisons, which is
large. They are read as the strength of the evidence, together with the
counts they come from: 6 seeds to none ($p = 0.03$) is reported as such, an
indication, not as ``significant''. The strongest results have $p$ of 0.002
or less, small enough to survive a correction over every comparison in their
experiment.

\subsection{Traps, and how they were handled}
\label{sec:traps}

\citet{nehmzow2009} lists the traps of experiments with robots. Each met this
project at least once:
\begin{description}
  \item[Confounding effects.] The first comparison of the models was run on
    the map the hybrid had been tuned on, against two models taken as
    published: it measured the tuning as much as the models. The others were
    then tuned on the same map, by one protocol fixed in advance (\cref{sec:models}),
    and the comparison moved to held-out maps. A residual confound remains:
    the hybrid had a whole development phase of tuning, the others 15 runs
    each. Earlier, the first sweep mixed flock size with starting density,
    since A had a fixed size; the spawn now places every flock at the same
    density.
  \item[Floor and ceiling effects.] On \texttt{l\_turn} two models succeed in
    every run with the plan: the map can't rank them. \texttt{dead\_end}
    separates them, so the second experiment runs there. The $j = 0$
    control measures the floor.
  \item[Systematic errors.] Every model and setting shares the same spawns,
    the same robot (one flight layer, one $\tau$ and $a_{\max}$) and the same
    measures, computed by one script from the referee's ground truth.
  \item[Wishful thinking.] Hypotheses were written before the runs, and results
    against them were kept: a retune that was expected to help Couzin and
    didn't, and a time limit that cost the hybrid most of its successes on
    \texttt{dead\_end}. That limit was questioned as too hard and checked
    rather than loosened: at the limit only the hybrid's leaders were in C,
    its followers 15--23\,m behind and out of perception, lost rather than
    slow. A failure was explained by replaying it before anything changed in
    the code, and a fix that changed what a model is, such as making Couzin's
    leaders wait for their flock, was turned down.
\end{description}
